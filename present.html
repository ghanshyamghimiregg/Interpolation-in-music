Overview

Draw a curve through a few notes. Hear it, don't just read it.

Place control points on a pitch-time grid, pick an interpolation method, and the curve plays back as a real glide — so the difference between Linear, Lagrange, and Cubic Spline is something you hear, not just see.

Formula

Natural cubic spline

Piecewise cubics with continuous second derivative (C²). Solved as a tridiagonal system with natural end conditions.

S₀(x) = a₀ + b₀(x−x₀) + c₀(x−x₀)² + d₀(x−x₀)³

Motivation

Why turn curves into sound?

Interpolation is normally taught as a static picture — a polynomial, an error number, an oscillating line. Pitch is frequency, frequency is logarithmic, and a glide between two notes is exactly the continuous function interpolation computes. So the question stops being "which method has lower error" and becomes "which one sounds natural."

What I had to solve

Six problems, six fixes

Runge's phenomenon felt abstract

Lagrange curves through many points overshoot wildly between them, but a graph alone doesn't make that feel like a real problem.

Fix → you hear the pitch shoot to extremes

"Smooth on a graph" ≠ smooth to the ear

Linear interpolation looks fine plotted, but a glide from straight segments sounds like a machine snapping direction.

Fix → spline glide sounds like a real portamento

Newton forward needs equal spacing

Students forget the constraint, then wonder why their difference table breaks on uneven notes.

Fix → app detects spacing, falls back automatically

Oscillator synths sounded fake

No amount of ADSR or formant tuning stopped every instrument from sounding like the same synthetic tone.

Fix → switched to real recorded instrument samples

A metronome-like clicking crept in

Noise-burst attack transients on every note lined up rhythmically into a phantom click track.

Fix → removed the noise bus, kept the sample's natural attack

All instruments blurred into one blip

Tuning sustain uniformly collapsed piano, violin, and flute into the same short envelope.

Fix → identity now comes from the recording, not the ADSR

The mathematics

Same six notes, three sounds

Lagrange, Newton's divided differences, and Newton's forward difference all build the same single polynomial through the points — they only differ in how that polynomial is computed. So there are really three audibly distinct shapes:

Linear · Lagrange/Newton family · Cubic spline — same 6 points
Method Continuity What you hear
Linear C0 Straight segments — abrupt turns at each note, mechanical
Lagrange C∞ on interval, unbounded between points Musical with few notes, wild pitch swings with many — Runge's phenomenon, audible
Newton divided difference same as Lagrange Identical sound — same polynomial, built incrementally
Newton forward/backward same as Lagrange Identical sound, only valid on evenly spaced notes
Natural cubic spline C2 Continuous acceleration in pitch — closest to a real musician's slide

Runge's phenomenon

Where the polynomial method breaks

Extend the same polynomial slightly past the first and last note and it swings far outside the pitch range you actually played. Spacing the notes at Chebyshev positions instead of evenly pulls the swing back in.

Equally spaced notes — visible overshoot
Chebyshev-spaced notes — overshoot pulled back in

Audio engine

From curve to instrument

The pitch-time curve doesn't drive a synthesizer — it drives a real recording. Each interpolated value becomes a pitch-shift delta applied to a sampled instrument note as it plays.

Oscillator engine

Sine/sawtooth waves shaped with ADSR. Every instrument converges toward the same synthetic tone; glides sound mechanical. Kept only as an offline, educational fallback.

Sample engine (WebAudioFont)

Real FluidR3_GM recordings — piano, violin, flute, sax, guitar, clarinet, synth brass. Bow attack, body resonance, and harmonic evolution come from the recording itself.

Takeaways

What this project shows

  • Numerical methods aren't abstract — they have consequences you can perceive.
  • Cubic spline wins both ways: C2 continuity mathematically, natural glides perceptually.
  • Runge's phenomenon is real and audible, not just a warning in a textbook.
  • Node spacing matters as much as method choice — Chebyshev nodes tame the overshoot.
  • Real samples beat synthesis: instrument identity lives in the recording, not the envelope.
